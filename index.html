Part I

Multiple Regression & Model Diagnostics

Foundations of multi-factor econometric modeling: Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), the Gauss-Markov theorem, coefficient inference, ANOVA decomposition, and confidence vs. prediction intervals.

01 Multiple Regression

Multiple Linear Regression & Classic Assumptions

The standard multiple linear regression model relates a dependent variable $Y$ to $k$ independent explanatory variables $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_k$:

$$Y_i = b_0 + b_1 X_{1i} + b_2 X_{2i} + \dots + b_k X_{ki} + \epsilon_i$$

Under the Gauss-Markov Theorem, OLS estimators are BLUE (Best Linear Unbiased Estimators), achieving minimum variance among all unbiased linear estimators when six classical assumptions hold:

  1. Linearity in Parameters: The relationship between $Y$ and the parameters is linear.
  2. No Perfect Collinearity: No exact linear relationship exists between two or more independent variables.
  3. Zero Expected Disturbance: $\mathbb{E}(\epsilon_i | X) = 0$.
  4. Homoskedasticity: Constant variance of errors: $\text{Var}(\epsilon_i) = \sigma^2$.
  5. No Serial Correlation: $\text{Cov}(\epsilon_i, \epsilon_j) = 0$ for all $i \neq j$.
  6. Normality of Errors: $\epsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ (required for finite-sample hypothesis testing).
Partial Regression Coefficient Interpretation: Each slope coefficient $\hat{b}_j$ represents the expected change in $Y$ for a 1-unit increase in $X_j$, holding all other $k-1$ independent variables strictly constant.
02 Hypothesis Testing

Hypothesis Testing, t-Distribution & Confidence Intervals

To test whether a factor significantly impacts asset returns, we test the null hypothesis $H_0: b_j = B_0$ (typically $B_0 = 0$) against $H_a: b_j \neq B_0$ using the Student's $t$-statistic with $n - k - 1$ degrees of freedom:

$$t = \frac{\hat{b}_j - B_0}{s_{\hat{b}_j}}, \quad df = n - k - 1$$

A $(1 - \alpha)$ confidence interval for the true population coefficient is constructed as:

$$\hat{b}_j \pm t_{c, \, \alpha/2} \times s_{\hat{b}_j}$$
03 ANOVA Mechanics

ANOVA Decomposition: SST, SSR, SSE & Adjusted R²

Total variation in $Y$ is decomposed via the Analysis of Variance identity:

$$\text{SST} = \text{SSR} + \text{SSE} \implies \sum (Y_i - \bar{Y})^2 = \sum (\hat{Y}_i - \bar{Y})^2 + \sum e_i^2$$

The standard coefficient of determination $R^2 = \frac{\text{SSR}}{\text{SST}} = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}}$ never decreases when new variables are added, even if irrelevant. To penalize model bloat, Adjusted $\bar{R}^2$ imposes a degrees-of-freedom penalty:

$$\bar{R}^2 = 1 - \left[ \frac{n - 1}{n - k - 1} (1 - R^2) \right]$$
Exam Key Fact: $\bar{R}^2$ is always $\le R^2$, can become negative when a model has weak explanatory power and high $k$, and only increases if the added regressor's $|t| > 1$.
04 F-Test Significance

Joint Hypothesis Testing & The F-Statistic

While individual $t$-tests evaluate one coefficient at a time, the F-test conducts a joint hypothesis test determining whether all slope coefficients are simultaneously zero:

$$H_0: b_1 = b_2 = \dots = b_k = 0 \quad \text{vs.} \quad H_a: \text{At least one } b_j \neq 0$$ $$F = \frac{\text{MSR}}{\text{MSE}} = \frac{\text{SSR} / k}{\text{SSE} / (n - k - 1)}$$

The F-statistic follows an asymmetric F-distribution with $(k, n - k - 1)$ degrees of freedom. Because it measures whether the regression explains significantly more variance than random noise, it is strictly a one-tailed test evaluated in the right tail.

05 Forecasting

Prediction Intervals vs. Confidence Intervals

Given forecasted regressor values $X_{1,f}, \dots, X_{k,f}$, the point forecast is $\hat{Y}_f = \hat{b}_0 + \sum \hat{b}_j X_{j,f}$. However, the uncertainty around this prediction depends critically on what is being forecasted:

  • Confidence Interval for Conditional Mean $E(Y|X_f)$: Uncertainty arises only from parameter estimation error $s_{\hat{Y}}^2 = \text{MSE} \left[ \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(X_f - \bar{X})^2}{\sum (X_i - \bar{X})^2} \right]$.
  • Prediction Interval for an Individual Observation $Y_f$: Must account for both parameter estimation error AND future random error variance: $s_f^2 = \text{MSE} \left[ 1 + \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(X_f - \bar{X})^2}{\sum (X_i - \bar{X})^2} \right]$.
Exam Rule: Prediction intervals are always wider than confidence intervals for the conditional mean due to the extra $+1 \times \text{MSE}$ term under the square root. Both expand hyperbolically as $X_f$ moves away from sample mean $\bar{X}$.
Part II

Model Misspecification & Econometric Violations

The core diagnostic triad tested on CFA Level II: Heteroskedasticity, Serial Correlation, and Multicollinearity. Detection algorithms, consequences on OLS inference, and robust covariance corrections.

06 Heteroskedasticity

Heteroskedasticity: Breusch-Pagan & White Robust SE

Heteroskedasticity occurs when the variance of the error term is non-constant across observations. Unconditional heteroskedasticity creates no major inference issues, but conditional heteroskedasticity (where error variance correlates with independent variables) breaks classical inference:

  • Consequences: OLS parameter estimates $\hat{b}_j$ remain unbiased and consistent, but OLS standard errors $s_{\hat{b}_j}$ are understated. Understated standard errors artificially inflate $t$-statistics and $F$-statistics, causing Type I errors (false discoveries).
  • Detection: The Breusch-Pagan test regresses squared OLS residuals $e_i^2$ on the independent variables: $$\text{BP Statistic} = n \times R^2_{\text{resid}} \sim \chi^2_k$$
  • Remedy: Compute White's heteroskedasticity-consistent (robust) standard errors or use Generalized Least Squares (GLS). Never re-estimate with unadjusted OLS!
07 Serial Correlation

Serial Correlation: Durbin-Watson & Newey-West SE

When residuals correlate across time ($\epsilon_t = \rho \epsilon_{t-1} + u_t$), the regression suffers from Autocorrelation. Common in financial time series with momentum or macroeconomic persistence:

  • Consequences: Like heteroskedasticity, coefficients remain unbiased and consistent, but standard errors are severely understated when positive serial correlation ($\rho > 0$) exists, inflating $t$-statistics and invalidating $F$-tests.
  • Detection: The Durbin-Watson statistic: $$\text{DW} \approx 2(1 - r) \quad \text{where } r \text{ is sample residual correlation}$$ If $\text{DW} < d_L$, reject $H_0$ in favor of positive serial correlation. If $d_L \le \text{DW} \le d_U$, the test is inconclusive. If $\text{DW} \approx 2.0$, no serial correlation exists.
  • Remedy: Compute Newey-West (Hansen-White) standard errors, which adjust for both serial correlation and heteroskedasticity. Note: White standard errors do NOT correct for serial correlation!
08 Multicollinearity

Multicollinearity: Variance Inflation Factor & Remedies

Multicollinearity arises when two or more independent variables are highly (though not perfectly) correlated. For example, simultaneously including Fama-French SMB and HML when value and size factors co-move:

  • Consequences: Coefficients remain unbiased and consistent, but standard errors $s_{\hat{b}_j}$ are inflated. Regressors lose individual statistical significance despite the overall regression model being highly predictive.
  • Classic Symptom: High $R^2$ and highly significant overall $F$-statistic, but low/insignificant individual $t$-statistics across the collinear variables.
  • Detection: Pairwise correlation $> 0.7$ (sufficient but not necessary); Variance Inflation Factor: $$\text{VIF}_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2} \quad (\text{VIF} > 5 \text{ or } 10 \text{ signals severe multicollinearity})$$
  • Remedy: Drop one of the redundant collinear variables, acquire more sample data, or use dimensionality reduction (Ridge regression or PCA).
09 Model Misspecification

Omitted Variable Bias & Functional Form Errors

Unlike heteroskedasticity, serial correlation, or multicollinearity (where OLS coefficients remain unbiased), omitting a relevant independent variable renders OLS estimators both biased and inconsistent if the omitted variable correlates with any included regressor:

$$\text{Bias}(\hat{b}_1) = \beta_2 \times \frac{\text{Cov}(X_1, X_2)}{\text{Var}(X_1)}$$
Exam Trap: If an omitted factor $X_2$ has a positive relationship with $Y$ ($\beta_2 > 0$) and correlates positively with included $X_1$ ($\text{Cov} > 0$), $\hat{b}_1$ suffers from positive upward bias. If one is negative and one positive, downward bias occurs!

10 Dummy Variables

Qualitative Regressors: Dummy Variables & Shifts

Dummy variables take values of 0 or 1 to capture qualitative attributes or regime shifts (e.g., Bull vs. Bear market, pre-crisis vs. post-crisis). To model $M$ mutually exclusive categories, include exactly $M - 1$ dummy variables to avoid the Dummy Variable Trap (perfect multicollinearity with intercept $b_0$).

Dummies can alter regression geometry in two ways:

  • Intercept Dummy ($b_2 D$): Causes a parallel vertical shift of the regression line.
  • Slope Dummy / Interaction Term ($b_3 (X \times D)$): Alters the slope/sensitivity of $Y$ with respect to $X$, rotating the regression line.
11 Logistic Regression

Binary Dependent Variables: Logit & Odds Ratios

When modeling discrete qualitative outcomes (e.g., Bankruptcy vs. Solvency, Acquisition vs. Independence), the Linear Probability Model (LPM) produces predicted probabilities outside $[0, 1]$. Logistic Regression (Logit) resolves this via the sigmoid link function:

$$P(Y = 1) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 X)}}$$

The transformation linearizes the log-odds (logit):

$$\ln\left(\frac{P}{1 - P}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X \implies \text{Odds Ratio (OR)} = e^{\beta_1}$$

For every 1-unit increase in $X$, the odds of the event occurring are multiplied by $e^{\beta_1}$. Logit models are estimated using Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) rather than OLS.

12 Influence Analysis

Influence Analysis: Leverage, Outliers & Cook's Distance

Not all outliers distort regression models equally. A regression analyst must distinguish between leverage and influence:

  • Outlier: An observation with an extreme residual $e_i$ in the dependent variable $Y$.
  • High Leverage Point: An observation with extreme values in the independent variables $X$ (measured by the diagonal element of the hat matrix $h_{ii}$).
  • Influential Observation: A point combining high leverage with a large studentized residual, unilaterally altering parameter estimates. Measured by Cook's Distance: $$D_i > 1.0 \quad \text{or} \quad D_i > \frac{4}{n} \implies \text{Highly Influential Observation}$$
13 Time-Series Trends

Time-Series Trends & Covariance Stationarity

Before estimating time-series regressions, an analyst must verify Covariance Stationarity. A time series is covariance stationary if it satisfies three strict properties:

  1. Constant, finite expected value over time: $\mathbb{E}(y_t) = \mu$ for all $t$.
  2. Constant, finite variance over time: $\text{Var}(y_t) = \sigma^2$ for all $t$.
  3. Covariance between any two periods depends solely on the distance between them (lag $s$), not calendar time: $\text{Cov}(y_t, y_{t-s}) = \gamma_s$.

If a series exhibits a deterministic trend, linear trends ($y_t = b_0 + b_1 t$) model constant absolute dollar increments, while log-linear trends ($\ln y_t = b_0 + b_1 t$) model constant percentage exponential growth.

14 Autoregressive Models

Autoregressive AR(1) Models & Mean Reversion

An Autoregressive model of order 1 ($AR(1)$) regresses a time series on its own immediate past value:

$$x_t = b_0 + b_1 x_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$$

For an $AR(1)$ series to be covariance stationary, its persistence coefficient must satisfy $|b_1| < 1$. Under this condition, the series exhibits a finite Mean Reverting Level:

$$x_L = \frac{b_0}{1 - b_1}$$

Forecasts are generated recursively via the Chain Rule of Forecasting:

$$\hat{x}_{t+1} = b_0 + b_1 x_t, \quad \hat{x}_{t+2} = b_0 + b_1 \hat{x}_{t+1} = b_0 + b_1 (b_0 + b_1 x_t)$$
15 Unit Roots

Random Walks, Unit Roots & Dickey-Fuller Testing

When $b_1 = 1$, the model becomes a Random Walk ($x_t = x_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$) and possesses a Unit Root. Its variance expands indefinitely ($\text{Var}(x_t) = t \sigma^2$), mean reversion is undefined ($x_L = 0/0$), and standard OLS $t$-tests produce spurious regressions (apparent statistical significance between completely unrelated series).

To detect a unit root, we apply the Dickey-Fuller (DF) test on the transformed regression:

$$x_t - x_{t-1} = b_0 + g x_{t-1} + \epsilon_t \quad (\text{where } g = b_1 - 1)$$ $$H_0: g = 0 \text{ (Unit Root / Non-Stationary)} \quad \text{vs.} \quad H_a: g < 0 \text{ (Stationary)}$$
Remedy: If a unit root is confirmed, transform the series by First-Differencing: $y_t = x_t - x_{t-1}$. An $I(1)$ series integrated of order 1 becomes an $I(0)$ stationary series upon differencing.
16 Cointegration & ARCH

Seasonality, Cointegration & ARCH Models

Two essential advanced time-series structures tested at Level II:

  • Cointegration: If two time series $X_t \sim I(1)$ and $Y_t \sim I(1)$ are individually non-stationary, but a linear combination $e_t = Y_t - \beta X_t \sim I(0)$ is stationary, the two series are Cointegrated. This reflects a true long-run economic equilibrium (e.g., spot and futures prices). Tested via the Engle-Granger test. Standard regression between them is valid without differencing!
  • Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity (ARCH): Financial returns exhibit volatility clustering where calm periods follow calm periods and crises follow crises: $$\sigma_t^2 = a_0 + a_1 e_{t-1}^2 + u_t$$ If $a_1 > 0$ and statistically significant, an $ARCH(1)$ process exists. OLS standard errors are invalid; Generalized Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity (GARCH) or ARCH estimation must be employed.
Part IV

Machine Learning & Big Data Projects

Modern quantitative workflows in asset management: Penalized regression (Ridge, Lasso), Supervised learners (Trees, Random Forests, SVM, KNN), Unsupervised clustering (PCA, k-Means), Performance evaluation (ROC-AUC), and Big Data NLP pipelines.

17 Penalized Regression

Penalized Regression: Ridge (L2) vs. Lasso (L1)

When estimating multi-factor investment models with hundreds of potential factors, standard OLS overfits and suffers from severe multicollinearity. Penalized regression adds a penalty term proportional to coefficient magnitude to the cost function:

  • Ridge Regression (L2 Penalty $\lambda \sum b_j^2$): Shrinks coefficients toward zero to reduce estimation variance. Due to spherical geometry, coefficients approach zero asymptotically but are never set strictly to zero. Retains all predictors.
  • Lasso Regression (L1 Penalty $\lambda \sum |b_j|$): Imposes a diamond-shaped constraint in parameter space. Forces uninformative coefficients to exactly zero, performing automated feature selection and producing sparse models.
  • Elastic Net: Combines both penalties ($\alpha L1 + (1 - \alpha) L2$) to handle groups of highly correlated factors.
18 Ensemble Learning

Decision Trees (CART) & Random Forests

Classification and Regression Trees (CART) partition feature space through recursive binary splits, maximizing purity at each split (measured via Gini Impurity $G = 1 - \sum p_i^2$ or Entropy). Unpruned trees have high variance and suffer from severe overfitting.

Ensemble methods overcome this limitation:

  • Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregating): Trains multiple trees in parallel on randomly resampled subsets of training data and averages predictions.
  • Random Forests: Extends bagging by sampling a random subset of features at each node split, decorrelating individual trees and significantly reducing model variance.
  • Boosting: Trains trees sequentially, with each subsequent learner focusing on the residual errors of prior trees (e.g., AdaBoost, Gradient Boosting).
19 Supervised Classifiers

Support Vector Machines (SVM) & k-Nearest Neighbors

Two essential non-linear supervised classifiers applied to equity selection and credit scoring:

  • Support Vector Machine (SVM): Identifies the optimal maximum-margin hyperplane separating investment classes. Support vectors are the critical data points lying closest to the boundary. The hyperparameter $C$ balances margin width against misclassification penalties. The kernel trick projects non-linear data into higher dimensional space.
  • k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN): A non-parametric, lazy learner that classifies a target based on majority vote among its $k$ closest neighbors in feature space. Small $k$ leads to high variance (overfitting); large $k$ leads to high bias (underfitting).
20 Unsupervised Learning

Unsupervised Learning: PCA & k-Means Clustering

Unsupervised algorithms uncover latent structures without target labels:

  • Principal Component Analysis (PCA): Transforms $k$ correlated financial regressors into linearly uncorrelated principal components ($PC_1, PC_2, \dots$) ordered by variance explained (eigenvalues). Eliminates multicollinearity while preserving total portfolio risk information. A scree plot locates the elbow cutoff.
  • k-Means Clustering: Partitions assets into $k$ non-overlapping clusters by iteratively assigning observations to the nearest centroid and recalculating centroid means until within-cluster sum of squares is minimized. Sensitive to initial centroid placement and scale.
21 Model Evaluation

Cross-Validation, Confusion Matrix & ROC-AUC

Evaluating machine learning models requires robust out-of-sample validation to prevent data snooping and overfitting. $K$-fold cross-validation splits data into $K$ partitions, iteratively training on $K-1$ and validating on 1.

For classification tasks, performance is evaluated using the Confusion Matrix:

  • Precision: $\frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FP}}$ (Percentage of positive predictions that are truly positive).
  • Recall (Sensitivity): $\frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FN}}$ (Percentage of actual positive cases correctly identified).
  • Specificity: $\frac{\text{TN}}{\text{TN} + \text{FP}}$ (True negative rate).
  • F1-Score: Harmonic mean balancing precision and recall: $2 \times \frac{\text{Precision} \times \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}$.
  • ROC Curve & AUC: Plots Sensitivity vs. $(1 - \text{Specificity})$ across all classification thresholds. AUC measures ranking discrimination ability ($\text{AUC} = 0.5$ is random, $1.0$ is perfect).
22 Big Data NLP

Big Data Financial Projects: Text Wrangling & NLP

Big Data in investment analysis spans Volume, Velocity, and Variety (particularly unstructured text from 10-K filings, earnings calls, and news feeds). The financial Natural Language Processing (NLP) pipeline comprises:

  1. Tokenization: Splitting raw text into individual word tokens.
  2. Normalization: Converting text to lowercase and removing punctuation/special characters.
  3. Stop Word Removal: Filtering high-frequency grammatical words ("the", "is", "at") carrying zero analytical signal.
  4. Lemmatization vs. Stemming: Lemmatization uses morphological dictionary vocabulary to reduce words to meaningful base roots (e.g., "expanding" $\to$ "expand"), whereas crude stemming chops word endings via heuristics.
  5. TF-IDF Vectorization: Weighs terms by local document frequency and inverse corpus frequency: $\text{TF-IDF} = \text{TF} \times \ln(N / \text{DF})$.
Financial Backtesting Biases: Big Data pipelines must strictly guard against lookahead bias (using data unavailable at time $t$), survivorship bias (excluding defunct firms), and data snooping bias (torturing data until spurious significance appears).
